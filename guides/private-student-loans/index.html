Private vs Government Student Loans: What to Know First

Compare private and government student loans on rates, protections, cosigners and refinancing, with notes for the US, Canada, the UK, India and Australia.

By Private.Finance Editorial Team·Updated October 30, 2026·7 min read·How we write & fact-check

Student loans are often the first large debt a person takes on, and the type of loan matters as much as the amount. Government and private loans can look similar on paper but behave very differently when money gets tight. This guide compares the two, explains when private borrowing can make sense, and highlights how the rules change from country to country.

Key takeaways

  • Government student loans usually come with built-in protections, such as income-linked repayment or payment pauses, that private loans rarely match.
  • Private loans are priced on credit, which often means needing a cosigner and choosing between fixed and variable rates.
  • A sensible order in most countries is grants and scholarships first, then government loans, then private loans only for any remaining gap.
  • Refinancing government loans into a private loan in the US can lower your rate but permanently gives up federal protections.
  • Student finance systems differ sharply between countries, so advice written for one market can be misleading in another.

How government and private student loans differ

Government student loans are a policy tool to widen access to education. Eligibility usually depends on enrolment and need, not credit history, and repayment often accounts for income. Private loans come from banks, credit unions and specialist lenders, and are priced on the borrower's (or cosigner's) credit profile.

Feature Typical government loan Typical private loan
Credit check Usually none or limited Required, often with a cosigner
Interest rate Set by law or policy; usually fixed or formula-based Set by lender; fixed or variable
Repayment flexibility Income-linked plans, deferment or pauses are common Limited; depends on lender policy
Forgiveness or write-off Available in some systems Rare
Death or disability discharge Commonly available Varies by lender and contract
Borrowing limits Capped by program rules Often up to the cost of attendance

Protections are the real difference

When a graduate earns less than expected, loses a job or falls ill, a government loan typically offers income-based payments, temporary pauses or, in some systems, eventual cancellation. A private lender may offer a short forbearance, but is not obliged to, and interest usually keeps accruing. That safety net has real value even though it does not appear in the headline rate.

Fixed vs variable rates

Private lenders usually offer a choice between fixed and variable rates. A fixed rate stays the same for the life of the loan, so payments are predictable. A variable rate is tied to a benchmark and moves with it, so it may start lower but can rise.

Worked example: fixed vs variable on a $30,000 loan

Consider a $30,000 private loan repaid over 10 years.

Scenario Rate Monthly payment Total interest over 10 years
Fixed rate 7.0% About $348 About $11,800
Variable rate, if it stays low 5.5% About $326 About $9,100
Variable rate, if it averages higher 8.5% About $372 About $14,600

The variable option could save about $2,700 if rates stay low, or cost about $2,800 more if they average higher. Tight budgets and long terms usually favour a fixed rate; a variable rate can be reasonable for a small balance you plan to repay quickly. The loan calculator can run your own numbers.

Cosigners: what they are agreeing to

Students often have little credit history, so private lenders frequently require a cosigner, usually a parent or relative. A cosigner is not a reference: they are legally responsible for the full debt if the student does not pay, and missed payments typically appear on both credit files.

Some lenders offer cosigner release after a set number of on-time payments and a credit review of the borrower. Criteria vary and approval is not guaranteed. A cosigner should also check what happens if either party dies, because contract terms differ.

When private student loans can make sense

Private loans are best treated as a gap-filler, not a first resort. They can be reasonable when:

  • You have used available grants, scholarships, work-study and government loans, and a funding gap remains.
  • The program has strong and fairly predictable earnings outcomes relative to the amount borrowed.
  • You or your cosigner have strong credit and can secure a competitive fixed rate.
  • You are studying abroad or in a program that government aid does not cover.

A common rule of thumb is to keep total student debt at or below your expected first-year salary. It does not guarantee affordability, but it keeps borrowing in proportion to likely income.

Refinancing: lower rate, fewer protections

Refinancing replaces one or more existing loans with a new private loan, ideally at a lower rate. It can make sense for private loans, especially if your credit has improved since you first borrowed.

In the US, refinancing federal student loans with a private lender is a one-way decision. The new private loan no longer qualifies for federal benefits such as income-driven repayment, federal deferment and forbearance, or public service and other forgiveness programs. Federal programs and borrowing limits have also changed significantly in recent years, so check the current rules with the official federal student aid office before deciding. Refinancing federal loans is generally safer only for borrowers with high, stable income, a solid emergency fund and no plans to rely on federal programs.

How student loans work in other countries

Canada

Canada Student Loans are federal, usually paired with a provincial or territorial loan. Interest on the federal portion was permanently eliminated in 2023, though some provinces still charge interest on their portion. Repayment typically begins after a six-month non-repayment period, and the Repayment Assistance Plan can reduce or pause payments based on income. Quebec runs its own program. Private options are mostly bank student lines of credit, usually variable-rate and tied to prime.

United Kingdom

UK government student loans are income-contingent. Repayments are typically 9% of earnings above a set threshold, collected through the tax system, and any remaining balance is written off after a fixed number of years depending on your repayment plan. Interest is linked to inflation measures. Thresholds and terms differ between England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland and between plan types. Commercial student loans are uncommon in the UK and do not share these features.

India

Most Indian students borrow through education loans from banks or non-bank lenders. Larger loans typically require collateral or a guarantor, and repayment usually begins after a moratorium covering the course plus a short grace period. Under Section 80E of the Income Tax Act, interest on qualifying education loans can generally be deducted for up to eight years under the old tax regime. Government interest subsidy and credit guarantee schemes exist for eligible students.

Australia

Australia's HELP loans are repaid through the tax system once income passes a threshold. Balances are indexed rather than charged commercial interest.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Taking a private loan before exhausting grants, scholarships and government loans.
  • Comparing loans on monthly payment alone.
  • Choosing a variable rate on a long repayment term without a plan for rising payments.
  • Treating cosigning as a formality rather than a legal obligation.
  • Refinancing government loans without understanding which protections you will lose.

Frequently asked questions

Do private student loans affect my credit score?

Yes. Private student loans are reported to credit bureaus like other loans, so on-time payments help build a record and missed payments harm it. In some countries, such as the UK, government loans repaid through the tax system generally do not appear on credit files in the same way.

Can student loans be discharged in bankruptcy?

In the US, both federal and private student loans are difficult to discharge in bankruptcy and usually require proving undue hardship. Other countries have their own rules; Canada, for example, typically imposes a waiting period after you leave school. Seek advice from a licensed insolvency professional or attorney.

Should I choose a fixed or variable rate?

A fixed rate suits most borrowers who want predictable payments over a long term. A variable rate may cost less if rates stay low and you repay quickly, but payments can rise later.

Is it worth paying interest while still in school?

If you can afford it, paying interest during study can stop it from being added to your balance and reduce your total cost. Prioritize this only after covering essential living costs and a basic emergency buffer.
