Private Wealth Management: Services, Fees and How to Choose

What private wealth management covers, who it suits, how firms charge, and how a small difference in annual fees can compound into a large sum over 25 years.

By Private.Finance Editorial Team·Updated October 30, 2026·7 min read·How we write & fact-check

Private wealth management is a bundled advisory service for people whose finances have outgrown a simple brokerage account and a yearly tax return. It brings investment management, tax planning, estate planning and sometimes family governance under one relationship. This guide covers what you receive, what it typically costs and how to judge a firm.

Key takeaways

  • Private wealth management combines portfolio management with tax, estate, insurance and family planning, usually for clients with roughly $1 million or more in investable assets, though minimums vary widely.
  • Most firms charge a percentage of assets under management (AUM), commonly around 0.5% to 1.5% a year on a sliding scale, but flat-fee and hourly models also exist.
  • Whether an advisor is legally required to act in your best interest depends on how they are registered and which country you live in.
  • Small differences in annual fees compound: on a $1 million portfolio over 25 years, the gap between a 1% and a 0.5% fee can exceed half a million dollars.
  • Evaluate a firm on its legal duty, total cost, conflicts of interest and depth of planning, not on its office or brand.

What private wealth management includes

The term has no single legal definition, so services differ from firm to firm. Most offerings combine some or all of the following.

Investment management

The core service is building and running a portfolio aligned with your goals, time horizon and risk tolerance: asset allocation, fund or security selection, rebalancing and monitoring. Larger firms may add separately managed accounts, direct indexing for tax-loss harvesting, or access to private funds.

Tax planning

Good wealth managers coordinate investments with tax rules: which assets to hold in which accounts, when to realise gains or losses, and how to draw retirement income. Priorities vary by country. A US client might focus on Roth conversions; a Canadian client on RRSP, TFSA and RRIF sequencing; a UK client on ISA and pension allowances; an Indian client on equity versus debt fund taxation; an Australian client on superannuation contribution caps. Many firms work alongside an outside accountant.

Estate and succession planning

This covers wills, trusts, powers of attorney, beneficiary designations and business succession, usually coordinated with a lawyer. Rules differ significantly: the UK levies inheritance tax, the US has a federal estate tax with a high exemption, Canada taxes deemed capital gains at death, and India and Australia currently have no separate inheritance tax.

Family office services

At the upper end, firms offer consolidated reporting across entities, bill paying, philanthropy and next-generation education. A single-family office serves one family and generally only makes economic sense at very high wealth levels; a multi-family office shares that infrastructure across several families.

Who it is for

Minimums are typically around $1 million or more in investable assets, though some firms start at $250,000 and others require $10 million or more. The service tends to add the most value when your situation is complex:

  • You own a business or hold concentrated company stock.
  • You have assets, income or heirs in more than one country.
  • You face meaningful estate or inheritance tax exposure.
  • You are nearing retirement and need a withdrawal strategy across several account types.

If your finances are straightforward, a flat-fee planner or a low-cost index portfolio may deliver most of the benefit for a fraction of the price.

How firms charge

Fee model How it works Typical range Best suited to
Percentage of AUM Annual fee on assets managed, often billed quarterly Roughly 0.5% to 1.5%, declining as assets rise Clients wanting ongoing, hands-off management
Flat or retainer fee Fixed annual fee regardless of portfolio size Varies widely; often several thousand to tens of thousands per year Larger portfolios where a percentage fee would be high
Hourly Pay for time spent on specific questions Varies by market and credentials Self-directed investors wanting occasional advice
Commission Paid by product providers when you buy Embedded in product costs Generally the most conflicted model

AUM fees are usually tiered. An illustrative schedule might charge 1.0% on the first $1 million, 0.75% on the next $4 million and 0.5% above $5 million. On a $3 million portfolio, that works out to $10,000 plus $15,000, or $25,000 a year, which is a blended rate of about 0.83%.

Fund expense ratios, trading, custody and product charges sit on top of the advisory fee, so ask for an all-in figure.

Fiduciary duty versus suitability

The legal standard your advisor operates under matters as much as the fee.

  • United States: Registered investment advisers owe clients a fiduciary duty. Broker-dealers follow Regulation Best Interest, which is stronger than the old suitability standard but is not identical to a fiduciary duty. Many professionals are dually registered and may switch roles depending on the account.
  • Canada: Registered firms must put clients' interests first when making suitability determinations under the Client Focused Reforms, but there is no universal statutory fiduciary duty for all advisors.
  • United Kingdom: Firms are regulated by the Financial Conduct Authority and must meet the Consumer Duty. Advice is labelled either independent or restricted.
  • India: SEBI separates registered investment advisers, who charge fees for advice, from mutual fund distributors, who earn commissions.
  • Australia: Personal financial advisers must act in the client's best interests under the Corporations Act, and many forms of conflicted remuneration are banned.

Ask any prospective firm to confirm in writing which standard applies to every account and service you will use.

Worked example: the cost of fee drag

Suppose you invest $1,000,000 earning 7% a year before advisory fees, with the fee treated as a reduction in annual return:

Annual advisory fee Net annual return Value after 25 years
0% (for reference) 7.0% $5,427,433
0.5% 6.5% $4,827,699
1.0% 6.0% $4,291,871

The difference between the 0.5% and 1% fee is about $535,800, and the 1% scenario forgoes roughly $1.1 million compared with no fee. Real returns vary year to year, but the principle holds: a small-sounding fee is charged every year on an ever-larger balance.

A higher fee can still be worth paying if the firm's tax planning saves more than the extra cost or prevents a costly mistake. The question is whether that value is demonstrable.

How to evaluate a firm

  1. Verify registration. Check the firm and advisors on your national regulator's public register, including disciplinary history.
  2. Get the all-in cost. Request a written estimate of advisory, fund and product costs in percentage and currency terms.
  3. Understand conflicts. Ask whether the firm earns anything from products it recommends, including proprietary funds or referrals.
  4. Check custody. Assets should sit with an independent, regulated custodian, not with the advisor directly.
  5. Assess planning depth. Ask for a sample plan and quarterly report, and look for specific recommendations rather than generic material.
  6. Review privacy practices. Ask what data the firm collects, who it shares it with and how it is secured.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Comparing headline advisory fees while ignoring fund and product costs.
  • Assuming a well-known name guarantees a fiduciary relationship.
  • Paying for comprehensive planning but receiving only portfolio management.
  • Failing to renegotiate fees as assets pass a tier threshold.
  • Overlooking cross-border tax issues if you have ties to more than one country.

Frequently asked questions

How much money do I need for private wealth management?

Minimums are commonly around $1 million in investable assets, but range from a few hundred thousand to tens of millions. Some firms accept smaller accounts from clients whose wealth is likely to grow, such as business owners.

Is a 1% fee reasonable?

A 1% fee is common for portfolios around $1 million, but its value depends on what is included. Genuine tax, estate and retirement planning may justify it. If you receive only a model portfolio, lower-cost alternatives are worth considering.

Can I negotiate wealth management fees?

Often, yes, particularly for larger portfolios. Ask whether household assets can be aggregated to reach a lower fee tier.

What is the difference between a wealth manager and a financial planner?

A financial planner typically focuses on building a plan, which may or may not include managing your investments. A wealth manager usually provides ongoing investment management combined with planning services. The titles are loosely regulated in many countries, so focus on registration, duty and services rather than the label.
