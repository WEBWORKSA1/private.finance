Credit Score Basics: How Scores Work and How to Improve Yours

Understand the factors behind credit scores, how score ranges differ in the US, Canada, UK, India and Australia, and practical steps to build or repair credit.

By Private.Finance Editorial Team·Updated October 30, 2026·7 min read·How we write & fact-check

A credit score is a number that summarizes how you have handled borrowed money, and lenders use it to decide whether to approve you and at what price. It can affect mortgage rates, rental applications and even some insurance and utility deposits. This guide explains what goes into a score, why the numbers look different from country to country, and how to improve yours steadily.

Key takeaways

  • Payment history and how much of your available credit you use are the two biggest drivers of most credit scores.
  • Score ranges are not universal: a "good" number in one country or with one agency can mean something different elsewhere.
  • Keeping card balances low relative to your limits can improve a score relatively quickly.
  • Negative marks such as late payments fade over time, but they typically stay on your file for several years.
  • Checking your own report does not hurt your score, and reviewing it regularly helps you catch errors and fraud.

What a credit score actually measures

Credit bureaus (also called credit reference agencies or credit information companies) collect data from lenders about your balances, limits, payments and any defaults. A scoring model turns that file into a number estimating how likely you are to fall seriously behind on a debt.

  • Credit report: the detailed record of your accounts held by each bureau.
  • Credit score: a number calculated from that report using a particular model.
  • Lender decision: lenders also weigh income, existing debts and their own criteria, so a good score helps but does not guarantee approval.

Because each bureau may hold slightly different data, it is normal to have several different scores at once.

The main factors behind your score

Scoring models are proprietary, but the widely used US FICO model publishes approximate weightings, and most models around the world look at similar behaviour.

Factor Approximate FICO weight What helps
Payment history 35% Paying every account on time, every month
Amounts owed (including utilization) 30% Keeping balances low relative to limits
Length of credit history 15% Keeping older accounts open and in good standing
New credit 10% Applying for new credit only when needed
Credit mix 10% Handling different types of credit responsibly over time

Payment history

A single payment that is reported as seriously late can drag a score down noticeably. In the US, lenders typically report a payment as late once it is 30 days past due, and severity increases at 60 and 90 days. Defaults, collections and court judgments have a larger impact still.

Utilization

Utilization is the share of your revolving credit limits, mainly credit cards and lines of credit, that you are using. Lower is generally better. Many lenders and educators suggest staying below roughly 30% overall, and people with the highest scores often keep it well under that.

Length of history, mix and new credit

A longer track record gives the model more evidence, so closing your oldest card can hurt. Each credit application usually creates a hard inquiry, which may lower a score slightly for a short time. Credit mix matters least, and it is never worth borrowing just to improve it.

Worked example: how utilization changes your score inputs

Suppose you have three credit cards:

Card Credit limit Balance reported Card utilization
Card A $5,000 $2,500 50%
Card B $3,000 $1,500 50%
Card C $2,000 $500 25%
Total $10,000 $4,500 45%

Your overall utilization is $4,500 ÷ $10,000 = 45%, which most models view as high.

Now suppose you pay $2,000 toward the cards before the statement date, bringing Card A down to $1,000 and Card B down to $1,000. Your total balance falls to $2,500, and utilization drops to 25%. Each card is now at or below about 33%.

Two practical points come out of this:

  1. Most issuers report your balance as of the statement date, not the due date, so paying before the statement closes lowers the reported balance.
  2. Many models have no long memory for utilization, so scores often respond within one or two reporting cycles.

Score ranges differ by country

There is no single global scale. Each country, and often each agency, uses its own range, and lenders may set their own cut-offs.

Country Main agencies or models Typical score range
United States FICO and VantageScore, using Equifax, Experian and TransUnion data 300 to 850
Canada Equifax and TransUnion Roughly 300 to 900
United Kingdom Experian, Equifax and TransUnion Each agency uses its own scale, for example up to 999 or 1,000
India TransUnion CIBIL, plus Experian, Equifax and CRIF High Mark CIBIL typically 300 to 900
Australia Equifax, Experian and illion Scales vary by agency, commonly up to 1,000 or 1,200

Compare your score only against the same agency's scale, and focus on behaviour rather than the exact number. In the UK in particular, lenders do not see the score shown in consumer apps; they apply their own models to your report.

How to build credit from scratch

If you are new to credit, or new to a country, you may have a "thin file" with too little data to generate a strong score.

  • Start with one simple account. A basic card with a low limit, or a secured card backed by a deposit, can begin your record.
  • Use it lightly and pay in full. A small recurring bill paid off every month shows reliable behaviour without building debt.
  • Register to vote where relevant. In the UK, being on the electoral roll at your address helps lenders confirm your identity.
  • Be patient with applications. Spacing out applications avoids multiple hard inquiries in a short period.
  • Check whether rent or utility payments can be reported. Some countries and services allow this, which can help thin files.

Credit history generally does not transfer between countries, though some lenders have programs for newcomers.

How to repair damaged credit

Repairing credit is mostly about consistent behaviour over time, not shortcuts.

  1. Get your reports from each bureau. Free reports are available in many countries, including the US, Canada, the UK and India.
  2. Dispute genuine errors. Bureaus are generally required to investigate disputes, and accurate negative information is not removable just because it hurts.
  3. Bring overdue accounts current. Stopping further late payments prevents new damage.
  4. Reduce revolving balances. This lowers utilization, which is often the fastest lever.
  5. Automate at least the minimum payment. This protects your payment history while you work on bigger balances.

Negative information typically stays on a credit file for roughly six to seven years in the US, Canada and the UK, with variation by item type and jurisdiction. Its impact usually weakens as it ages, especially when newer history is positive.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Closing your oldest credit card to "tidy up", which can shorten history and raise utilization.
  • Applying for several cards or loans in a short window.
  • Paying services that promise to erase accurate negative information.
  • Ignoring small bills that can end up in collections.
  • Assuming a score from one app or agency is the one your lender will see.

Frequently asked questions

Does checking my own credit score lower it?

No. Checking your own report or score is a soft inquiry and does not affect your score. Hard inquiries, which occur when you apply for credit, may have a small, temporary effect.

How long does it take to build a good credit score?

Many scoring systems need several months of account history before producing a score. Building a strong score typically takes a year or more of on-time payments and low utilization.

Should I carry a balance to build credit?

No. Carrying a balance and paying interest does not improve your score. Paying the statement balance in full shows the same behaviour without the cost.

Do debit cards or savings accounts build credit?

Generally not, because they do not involve borrowing and are not usually reported to credit bureaus. Some providers are beginning to include other data, such as rent payments.
