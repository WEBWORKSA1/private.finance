How to Choose a Fee-Only Financial Advisor

Fee-only vs fee-based vs commission advisors explained, the credentials to look for by country, how to verify registration, interview questions and red flags.

By Private.Finance Editorial Team·Updated October 30, 2026·7 min read·How we write & fact-check

Choosing a financial advisor is a consequential decision: the right one can improve years of choices, while the wrong one can quietly cost you through fees and conflicted advice. This guide explains how advisors are paid, which credentials matter in different countries, and how to vet one before you hand over any money.

Key takeaways

  • A fee-only advisor is paid solely by clients, not by product providers, which removes the most direct conflict of interest.
  • "Fee-based" sounds similar but usually means fees plus commissions, so ask directly how every dollar of compensation is earned.
  • Credentials such as the CFP, CFA and CPA/PFS indicate training and ethics obligations; equivalents exist in Canada, the UK, India and Australia.
  • Always verify registration and disciplinary history using your national regulator's public database.
  • A good first meeting focuses on your goals and situation, explains costs clearly and involves no pressure to buy anything.

Fee-only vs fee-based vs commission

Model How the advisor is paid Main conflict Typical cost structure
Fee-only Only by the client May favour keeping assets under management or adding services Percentage of assets, flat fee, retainer or hourly
Fee-based Client fees plus commissions on some products Incentive to sell commission-paying products Mix of fees and embedded product commissions
Commission-only By product providers Incentive to sell products and to trade more Commissions built into product costs

No model is conflict-free. An advisor charging a percentage of assets may hesitate to recommend paying off a mortgage, because that reduces assets under management. But fee-only conflicts are easier to see.

How the rules differ by country

  • United States: "fee-only" is not a regulated legal term, but professional bodies define it strictly. Registered investment advisers owe a fiduciary duty; broker-dealers follow Regulation Best Interest.
  • Canada: many advisors are paid through commissions embedded in mutual funds, though certain deferred sales charge arrangements have been banned. Fee-only planners exist but are less common.
  • United Kingdom: since the Retail Distribution Review in 2013, advisers can no longer receive commissions from providers for investment advice, so clients pay adviser charges directly. Advisers must state whether their advice is independent or restricted.
  • India: SEBI separates registered investment advisers, who charge fees and cannot earn commissions on products they recommend, from mutual fund distributors, who earn commissions. SEBI also caps the fees registered advisers can charge.
  • Australia: the Future of Financial Advice reforms banned many forms of conflicted remuneration, and use of the word "independent" is restricted to advisers meeting strict conditions.

Credentials to look for

Credentials are not guarantees, but they indicate training, examinations and an ongoing ethics commitment.

Common international credentials

  • CFP (Certified Financial Planner): a comprehensive planning credential requiring education, examination, experience and ethics. It is available in many countries through national bodies. In the US, CFP professionals must act as fiduciaries when providing financial advice under the CFP Board's standards.
  • CFA (Chartered Financial Analyst): a rigorous investment analysis credential, more focused on portfolio management than on broad personal planning.
  • CPA/PFS (Personal Financial Specialist): a US credential for certified public accountants who specialise in personal financial planning, useful where tax is central.

Country equivalents

  • Canada: the CFP and QAFP designations are administered by FP Canada. In Quebec, the Pl. Fin. title is used. Ontario and Saskatchewan have introduced title protection rules for "financial planner" and "financial advisor."
  • United Kingdom: look for Chartered Financial Planner status, a Chartered firm designation, or the CFP awarded through a UK professional body.
  • India: check for SEBI registration as an investment adviser. The CFP is also available locally.
  • Australia: advisers must meet minimum education and examination standards and be listed on the national register. Many also hold the CFP.

Be wary of impressive-sounding designations that require only a short online course.

How to verify registration

Every major market has a public way to check whether an advisor is licensed and whether they have a disciplinary record.

  1. Find the right regulator. In the US, investment advisers register with the SEC or state regulators, and brokers with FINRA. In Canada, check with the provincial securities regulator and CIRO. In the UK, the FCA maintains a public register. In India, SEBI publishes lists of registered investment advisers. In Australia, ASIC keeps the Financial Advisers Register.
  2. Search the individual and the firm. Confirm the name, registration number and the services they are authorised to provide.
  3. Review disciplinary history. Look for complaints, sanctions, arbitration or terminations.
  4. Read the disclosure documents. In the US, advisers file a Form ADV describing fees, services and conflicts. Other countries require similar fee and services disclosures. Read them before your first meeting.
  5. Verify credentials separately. Check the credential-issuing body's directory to confirm the designation is current.

Worked example: comparing the cost of three advisors

Suppose you have a $500,000 portfolio and need ongoing planning and investment management. Fees vary widely, so these numbers are illustrative.

Advisor Fee structure Annual cost at $500,000 Annual cost at $1,000,000
A: Assets under management 1% of assets $5,000 $10,000
B: Flat annual retainer $3,000 per year $3,000 $3,000
C: Hourly $250 per hour, 10 hours per year $2,500 $2,500

As the portfolio grows, the AUM fee rises with it while the others do not. Advisor A may still be worth it if they provide discretionary management and tax planning you would otherwise do poorly; Advisor C suits someone comfortable implementing advice themselves. Add fund expenses to find the true total cost.

Questions to ask in an interview

  1. Are you fee-only, and do you or your firm receive any third-party compensation?
  2. Will you act in my best interest at all times and for all accounts, in writing?
  3. What will I pay each year, in currency terms, including fund costs?
  4. What credentials and registration details do you hold?
  5. Who is your typical client, and how many clients do you serve?
  6. Which services are included: tax, retirement income, estate, insurance, cross-border?
  7. How do you decide on asset allocation?
  8. Who holds my assets, and will I see custodian statements directly?
  9. How do you protect client data, and do you share it with anyone?
  10. What happens if I want to leave?

Red flags

  • Reluctance to explain compensation clearly or in writing.
  • Promises of guaranteed or unusually high returns.
  • Pressure to decide quickly or move all your money at once.
  • Recommending complex products before understanding your situation.
  • Asking you to write cheques payable to the advisor personally rather than to an independent custodian.
  • A disciplinary record they did not mention.
  • Unclear or unverifiable credentials.

What a good first meeting looks like

A first meeting is typically free and lasts about an hour. The advisor should spend most of it listening to your goals, family, income, assets, debts, tax situation and attitude to risk, then explain their services, fees and legal duty plainly.

You should leave knowing what working together would cost and deliver, with no obligation. A good advisor will also tell you if your needs are simple enough not to require ongoing advice.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Assuming "fee-based" means the same as "fee-only."
  • Focusing only on investment returns instead of planning value and total cost.
  • Skipping the regulator lookup because the advisor works for a well-known firm.
  • Failing to review the relationship annually as your assets and needs change.

Frequently asked questions

Is a fee-only advisor always better?

Not always, but the fee-only model removes the most direct conflict: commissions from product providers. Quality still depends on competence, services and cost. A fee-only advisor charging well above market rates for limited service may be worse value than a transparent alternative.

How much does a fee-only financial advisor cost?

Costs vary by country and model. Percentage fees on managed assets commonly run around 1% a year for moderate portfolios, declining as assets grow, while flat-fee and hourly planners charge set amounts. Ask for a written quote in currency terms.

Do I need an advisor if I invest in index funds?

Not necessarily. Many people manage a simple index portfolio themselves. An advisor tends to add value for complex decisions such as retirement income, tax or cross-border issues.

Can I hire an advisor for a one-time plan?

Yes. Many fee-only planners offer one-off plans or hourly consultations, a cost-effective way to get a professional review without ongoing fees.
