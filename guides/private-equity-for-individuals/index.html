Private Equity for Individuals: How It Works and What to Weigh

How private equity works for individual investors: capital calls, the J-curve, 2-and-20 fees, semi-liquid funds, key risks and allocation factors.

By Private.Finance Editorial Team·Updated October 30, 2026·7 min read·How we write & fact-check

Private equity, once reserved for institutions and the very wealthy, is now being packaged for individual investors through lower-minimum and semi-liquid funds. The pitch is access to companies that are not listed on stock exchanges and the potential for higher long-term returns. This guide explains how private equity actually works, what it costs, and the considerations to weigh before adding it to a portfolio.

Key takeaways

  • Private equity funds buy stakes in private companies, aim to improve them, and sell them years later; your money is typically committed for a decade or more.
  • Traditional funds draw your money gradually through capital calls and return it through distributions, producing the early dip known as the J-curve.
  • The classic fee structure is "2 and 20": around a 2% annual management fee plus 20% of profits above a hurdle, which takes a large share of gross returns.
  • Evergreen and semi-liquid funds lower minimums and allow periodic withdrawals, but those withdrawals are limited and can be suspended.
  • Results vary widely between managers, so manager selection, cost and liquidity planning matter more than the asset class label.

Private equity versus public markets

Public markets let you buy shares in listed companies instantly, at a visible price, for very low cost. Private equity offers something different: ownership of companies that are not traded, often with active involvement in how they are run.

Feature Public equities Private equity
Liquidity Daily Typically locked up 10 years or more in traditional funds
Pricing Market price, updated continuously Periodic valuations by the manager
Minimum investment Price of one share or fund unit Often tens of thousands or more; lower in some newer vehicles
Typical costs Very low for index funds Management fees, performance fees and fund expenses
Transparency Extensive public disclosure Limited, periodic reporting
Return dispersion Index funds track the market closely Large gaps between top and bottom managers

Supporters argue that private owners can take a longer view and improve operations. Critics note that historical outperformance is debated, that reported valuations smooth volatility, and that net returns after fees sit much closer to public markets than gross figures suggest.

How a traditional private equity fund works

The structure

Most funds are limited partnerships or similar structures. The manager, called the general partner, makes investment decisions. Investors, called limited partners, provide the capital. A typical fund has a life of around 10 to 12 years, with the first few years used to invest and the rest to grow and sell companies.

Commitments and capital calls

You sign a commitment, say $100,000, and the fund calls portions of it as it finds investments, usually at short notice. Missing a capital call carries severe penalties, so you need cash available throughout the investment period.

Distributions

When the fund sells a company, it returns proceeds to investors as distributions. Most distributions come in the second half of the fund's life.

The J-curve

Early on, fees are charged while few companies have been sold, so your cumulative cash position falls before it rises, tracing the shape of a J.

Worked example: a $100,000 commitment

The table below is a hypothetical illustration of how cash might flow for an investor who commits $100,000, with distributions shown after fees and carried interest. It is not a forecast; many funds return less, and some lose money.

Year Capital called Distributions Cumulative net cash
1 $25,000 $0 -$25,000
2 $25,000 $0 -$50,000
3 $20,000 $5,000 -$65,000
4 $15,000 $15,000 -$65,000
5 $5,000 $30,000 -$40,000
6 $0 $35,000 -$5,000
7 $0 $40,000 $35,000
8 $0 $25,000 $60,000

In this scenario, the investor pays in $90,000 in total, since funds often do not call the full commitment, and receives $150,000 back. That is a net multiple of about 1.67 times the money invested and an internal rate of return of roughly 15% a year. Notice that the investor was out of pocket for six years before breaking even, and had up to $65,000 tied up at once.

Understanding 2 and 20

The traditional fee model has two parts:

  • Management fee: typically around 1.5% to 2% a year, often calculated on committed capital during the investment period and on invested capital afterwards.
  • Carried interest: typically 20% of profits, usually paid only once investors have received their capital back plus a preferred return, commonly around 8% a year. A catch-up provision often lets the manager then receive its full share.

Fund and transaction expenses can come on top. Over a fund's life, management fees alone on a $100,000 commitment can reach $15,000 or more, which is why gross and net returns can differ by several percentage points a year.

Evergreen and semi-liquid vehicles

To reach individual investors, managers have launched funds that stay open indefinitely, accept new money regularly and allow periodic redemptions.

  • United States: tender-offer funds, interval funds and non-traded vehicles, some open to non-accredited investors.
  • Europe: the European Long-Term Investment Fund, which was reformed to widen access.
  • United Kingdom: the Long-Term Asset Fund, plus listed private equity investment trusts that trade on the stock exchange.
  • India: Category II alternative investment funds, which typically require a minimum of ₹1 crore for most investors.
  • Canada and Australia: a mix of listed vehicles and unlisted funds generally offered to accredited or wholesale investors.

These vehicles invest immediately rather than through capital calls, reducing the J-curve. The trade-off is that redemptions are typically limited to a small percentage of the fund each quarter and can be restricted or suspended when many investors want out at once. Fees are often comparable to or higher than traditional funds.

Key risks

  • Illiquidity: you may be unable to access your money for years.
  • Valuation lag: reported values may trail market conditions, particularly in downturns.
  • Leverage: buyouts typically use substantial debt, magnifying gains and losses.
  • Manager dispersion: the gap between strong and weak managers is wide, and the strongest are often hard to access.
  • Fees: layered costs erode returns, particularly in fund-of-funds structures.
  • Tax complexity: reporting can be complicated and treatment varies by country and vehicle.

Allocation considerations

This is general information, not personal advice. Factors to weigh include:

  1. Liquidity needs: money you might need for a home, education or emergencies should not be locked up.
  2. Time horizon: private equity suits investors who can wait a decade or more.
  3. Existing exposure: if you already own a business or hold concentrated company shares, you may already carry significant private risk.
  4. Diversification: spreading commitments across several years and managers reduces the risk of a poorly timed vintage.
  5. Account type: in some countries, holding private assets in tax-advantaged retirement accounts is restricted or complex.
  6. Proportion: many investors who use private equity keep it to a modest share of their total portfolio, with the core in liquid, diversified holdings.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Committing capital without setting aside cash for future capital calls.
  • Comparing gross private equity returns with net public market returns.
  • Assuming semi-liquid funds offer the same liquidity as a mutual fund or ETF.
  • Concentrating in a single fund or vintage year.

Frequently asked questions

Can ordinary investors buy private equity?

Increasingly, yes. Listed private equity companies and trusts can be bought on a stock exchange, and some semi-liquid funds accept non-accredited investors. Traditional funds are still generally limited to investors who meet accredited, wholesale or similar thresholds set by national regulators.

Does private equity outperform the stock market?

Some studies and indices suggest it has historically, while others argue that outperformance shrinks or disappears after fees and adjustment for leverage. Results vary greatly by manager and time period. It is best treated as a possibility, not a given.

What happens if I miss a capital call?

Fund agreements typically impose strict penalties, which can include forfeiting part of your existing investment or being forced to sell your stake at a steep discount. Always keep enough liquid cash to meet your remaining commitment.

Is a semi-liquid fund a good alternative to a traditional fund?

It can be more practical because minimums are lower and money is invested immediately. However, withdrawals are capped and can be suspended, and fees can be high.
