Private banking is a relationship-based service that banks offer to clients who hold substantial deposits, investments or borrowing with them. In exchange for concentrating assets at one institution, clients typically receive a dedicated contact, tailored lending and preferential pricing. Whether that bargain is good value depends on what you give up, and this guide explains how to weigh it.

## Key takeaways

- Private banking pairs a dedicated relationship manager with customised banking, lending and often investment services, usually for clients with at least several hundred thousand to several million in assets at the bank.
- The most valuable perks are usually lending-related: securities-backed credit lines, flexible mortgages and faster credit decisions.
- The main trade-off is concentration: the bank profits from your deposits, loans and the products it sells you, which creates potential conflicts.
- Private banking, independent wealth management and premium retail banking tiers are different services with different incentives.
- Price every perk against what you would pay elsewhere before assuming the relationship is a good deal.

## What private banking is

A private bank is either a standalone institution or, more commonly, a division of a large bank that serves wealthier clients. The defining feature is the relationship: instead of calling a general helpline, you deal with a relationship manager who coordinates specialists in lending, investments, trusts and foreign exchange.

Typical services include higher-limit and multi-currency accounts, tailored mortgages, lending secured against portfolios, investment management (often through the bank's own platforms), trust and estate services, foreign exchange, and card, travel and concierge benefits.

## Minimums and eligibility

Entry requirements vary by bank and country. Some private banks look for roughly $1 million to $5 million in investable assets; others offer a "private client" tier starting at a few hundred thousand. In the UK, some banks accept a combination of income and assets. In India, private banking divisions generally set thresholds in crores of rupees, often combining deposits, investments and loans. Australian and Canadian banks similarly set their own criteria.

Banks often count the total relationship, including mortgages and business accounts, so ask how assets are aggregated.

## The relationship manager

Your relationship manager is your main point of contact. A good one understands your situation and gets things done quickly, such as approving a large transfer or arranging bridging finance before a property purchase.

Relationship managers are bank employees, and their performance is often measured partly on the revenue their client book generates. That does not make them untrustworthy, but their incentives are not purely aligned with yours. Ask how the bank ensures continuity if your manager leaves.

## Perks worth understanding

### Lending

Lending is where private banks often add the most value.

- **Securities-based lending** (called a Lombard loan in the UK and Europe) lets you borrow against a portfolio without selling it. Banks typically lend around half to two-thirds of the value of a diversified portfolio, and less against concentrated or volatile holdings. If markets fall, you may face a margin call and be forced to add cash or sell.
- **Custom mortgages** can accommodate irregular income, foreign income, high-value properties or interest-only structures that mainstream lenders decline.
- **Bridging and liquidity lines** can help with timing gaps, such as buying a home before selling the old one.

### Pricing

Private banks may offer relationship discounts on loan rates, better foreign exchange spreads and waived fees. These can be worthwhile, but deposit rates on standard accounts are sometimes lower than you could earn elsewhere.

### Service and convenience

Concierge services, lounge access and event invitations are pleasant but rarely financially significant.

## Trade-offs and conflicts of interest

### Proprietary products

Many private banks offer their own funds, structured products and insurance. These may be good, but the bank earns more when you choose them. Ask whether a recommended product is proprietary and how its costs compare with independent alternatives.

### Structured products

Structured notes combine a bond with derivatives to offer features such as capital protection or enhanced income. They can be complex, illiquid and carry embedded fees that are hard to see. They also carry the credit risk of the issuer.

### Cash drag

Banks profit from the gap between what they pay on deposits and what they earn lending them out. Large balances held in low-interest accounts can quietly cost you.

### Concentration risk

Deposit insurance has per-depositor limits. In the US, FDIC coverage is $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category. Canada's CDIC covers up to $100,000 per eligible category, India's DICGC covers up to ₹5 lakh per depositor per bank, and Australia's Financial Claims Scheme covers up to A$250,000 per account holder per institution. The UK's FSCS has its own limit, so check the current figure. Above these levels, deposits are exposed to the bank's own credit risk. Investments held in custody are generally kept separate from the bank's balance sheet, but it is worth confirming how they are held.

## Worked example: pricing the relationship

Imagine a bank requires you to hold $1,000,000 with it to qualify for private banking. It offers a mortgage rate discount of 0.25 percentage points on an $800,000 mortgage, and it asks you to keep $250,000 in a deposit account paying 1%, when a money market fund elsewhere might pay around 4%.

| Item | Annual effect |
|---|---|
| Mortgage discount: $800,000 x 0.25% | Save $2,000 |
| Waived fees and FX savings (estimate) | Save $1,000 |
| Lower deposit rate: $250,000 x (4% - 1%) | Lose $7,500 |
| Net effect | Lose $4,500 |

These numbers are hypothetical and rates change, but the exercise shows why perks must be weighed against the opportunity cost of balances the bank asks you to hold. Negotiate a better deposit rate or hold less cash, and the result may flip in your favour.

## Private bank vs wealth manager vs premium banking

| Feature | Private bank | Independent wealth manager | Premium retail tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| Typical entry point | Several hundred thousand to several million | Often around $1 million, varies | Tens of thousands to a few hundred thousand |
| Core strength | Lending, banking, custody | Investment and financial planning | Everyday banking perks |
| Lending | Extensive and tailored | Usually none directly | Standard products, small discounts |
| Product bias | May favour in-house products | Depends on fee model and ownership | Bank's own products |
| Legal duty | Varies by service and country | Often fiduciary if registered as an adviser | Generally sales and suitability rules |
| Main cost | Spreads, product fees, advisory fees | Advisory fees | Account fees or balance requirements |

Many households combine a private bank for lending with an independent advisor for investment advice.

## Questions to ask before signing up

1. What are the minimum balances, and which accounts count towards them?
2. What deposit rates will I receive compared with your public rates?
3. How are relationship managers paid and assessed?
4. Which investment products are proprietary, and what are their total costs?
5. What loan-to-value ratios apply to securities-based lending, and what triggers a margin call?
6. Are my investments held separately from the bank's own assets?
7. What personal data do you collect, and who do you share it with?
8. What fees apply if I move assets elsewhere?

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Joining for lifestyle perks without pricing the lower deposit rates.
- Keeping deposits well above insured limits at a single institution.
- Buying structured products without understanding the embedded costs and issuer risk.
- Borrowing heavily against a portfolio without planning for a market decline.
- Assuming the relationship manager's investment advice is independent.

## FAQ

### Is private banking worth it?

It can be if you regularly need tailored lending, large or international transactions, or complex account structures. If you mainly want investment advice, an independent advisor may offer better alignment.

### Can I negotiate with a private bank?

Yes. Loan rates, deposit rates and advisory fees are often negotiable, especially for larger relationships. Getting a competing quote for a mortgage or a securities-backed loan gives you a useful benchmark.

### Are private bank accounts safer than ordinary accounts?

Not inherently. Deposit insurance limits are generally the same as at the bank's retail arm. Safety depends on the bank's financial strength and on how your investments are held in custody.

### Do I have to invest with the bank to get private banking?

Not always, but many banks expect a meaningful share of your investable assets. Some accept relationships built mainly on borrowing or business banking.
