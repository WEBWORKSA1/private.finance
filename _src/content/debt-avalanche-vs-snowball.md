When you have several debts, deciding which one to attack first can feel as hard as finding the money to pay them. The two best-known strategies, the avalanche and the snowball, both work. They simply optimise for different things: one for the lowest total cost, the other for early momentum. This guide walks through both with real numbers so you can choose the approach you are most likely to stick with.

## Key takeaways

- Both methods require paying the minimum on every debt and sending all extra money to one target debt at a time.
- The avalanche method targets the highest interest rate first and usually costs the least in total interest.
- The snowball method targets the smallest balance first and delivers quicker wins, which can help motivation.
- The interest difference between the two is often modest, so the method you will follow consistently tends to matter more.
- Consolidation or a balance transfer can reduce costs, but only if fees, rates and spending habits are handled carefully.

## How both methods work

Both strategies share the same structure:

1. List every debt with its balance, interest rate and minimum payment.
2. Decide on a fixed total monthly amount you can put toward debt.
3. Pay the minimum on every debt so nothing falls behind.
4. Send every extra dollar to a single target debt.
5. When the target is paid off, roll its payment into the next target.

Each debt you clear frees up its payment, so the amount aimed at the next target grows over time. The only difference between the methods is the order.

### The avalanche method

The avalanche orders debts from highest interest rate to lowest. Because the most expensive balance shrinks first, less interest builds up overall. It is the mathematically optimal choice when the goal is the lowest total cost.

### The snowball method

The snowball orders debts from smallest balance to largest, regardless of rate. The first debt usually disappears quickly, which gives a visible win and reduces the number of bills to track. The trade-off is that a high-rate debt may keep accruing interest for longer.

## Worked example: three debts, two strategies

Assume you have three debts and can commit $800 per month in total:

| Debt | Balance | Interest rate (APR) | Minimum payment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Credit card | $6,000 | 22% | $150 |
| Personal loan | $1,500 | 10% | $50 |
| Car loan | $9,000 | 7% | $220 |

The minimums add up to $420, which leaves $380 extra each month for the target debt. For simplicity, the figures below assume interest compounds monthly, no new borrowing and fixed minimum payments.

| | Avalanche | Snowball |
|---|---|---|
| Payoff order | Credit card, personal loan, car loan | Personal loan, credit card, car loan |
| First debt cleared | Credit card, around month 13 | Personal loan, around month 4 |
| All debts cleared | Around month 23 | Around month 24 |
| Approximate total interest | About $1,780 | About $1,940 |

In this example, the avalanche saves roughly $160 and finishes about a month sooner. The snowball, however, eliminates its first debt nine months earlier. For some people, that early win is worth more than $160 because it keeps them engaged with the plan.

The gap grows when rates are far apart and the high-rate debt is also large, and shrinks when balances are small or rates are similar. Enter your own figures in the debt payoff calculator to see the gap in your situation.

## The behavioural side of debt payoff

Paying off debt usually takes months or years, and most plans fail because people stop following them, not because they picked the wrong order.

### Why the snowball appeals to many people

- Quick wins provide visible proof that the plan works.
- Fewer accounts means fewer due dates and less mental load.
- Each closed balance frees up cash flow that can absorb surprises.

### Why the avalanche appeals to others

- It minimises the total cost, which feels rewarding to people motivated by numbers.
- It deals with the most expensive debt, often a credit card, before it can grow.
- It reduces the long-term risk from high-rate balances if your income changes.

If you tend to lose interest without visible progress, the snowball's early wins may be worth the extra interest. If you are steady and numbers-driven, the avalanche usually fits better.

## Hybrid approaches

You do not have to choose one method rigidly. Common hybrids include:

- **Quick win, then avalanche:** clear one very small balance first for momentum, then switch to highest-rate order.
- **Rate threshold:** use the avalanche for any debt above a chosen rate, such as 15%, then use the snowball for the rest.
- **Cash-flow priority:** target the debt whose payoff frees up the biggest monthly payment, which can help if your budget is tight.
- **Deadline-driven:** prioritise a debt with an expiring promotional rate before the higher rate kicks in.

## Consolidation and balance transfers

Consolidation combines several debts into one, ideally at a lower rate. It can work alongside either method, but it is a tool, not a solution on its own.

### Debt consolidation loans

A personal loan used to pay off credit cards can replace several high-rate balances with one fixed payment and a clear end date. It only helps if the rate is meaningfully lower after fees and the cards are not run back up.

### Balance transfer cards

Balance transfer cards, available in markets such as the US, Canada, the UK and Australia, can offer a low or zero promotional rate for a set period. Typical costs and conditions include:

- A transfer fee, often a few percent of the amount moved.
- A promotional period that ends, after which a much higher standard rate applies.
- The need for good credit to qualify and a high enough limit to make it worthwhile.

A transfer helps most when you can realistically clear the balance before the promotion ends. Divide the transferred amount by the number of promotional months to find the monthly payment you would need.

### Home equity and secured options

Using home equity can lower the rate, but it turns unsecured debt into debt secured against your home, raising the stakes if you fall behind.

## Choosing the right approach for you

| If this describes you | Consider |
|---|---|
| Motivated by saving money and comfortable waiting for results | Avalanche |
| Need early wins to stay on track | Snowball |
| One debt has a much higher rate than the rest | Avalanche, or a rate-threshold hybrid |
| Many small balances creating stress and missed payments | Snowball |
| Good credit and a realistic plan to pay within a promotional window | Balance transfer plus either method |

A small emergency buffer helps keep unexpected expenses off your cards.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Skipping minimum payments on non-target debts, which leads to fees and credit damage.
- Not rolling freed-up payments into the next debt, which removes the method's main benefit.
- Continuing to add new charges to cards you are paying down.
- Consolidating without comparing fees, the true rate and the full repayment term.
- Ignoring a debt with an expiring promotional rate until the higher rate applies.

## FAQ

### Which method pays off debt faster?

The avalanche usually finishes slightly sooner and costs less in interest because it reduces the most expensive balances first. The snowball can match it closely when rates are similar. In practice, the method you follow consistently tends to be the fastest.

### Should I include my mortgage or student loans?

Many people focus these methods on consumer debts such as credit cards, personal loans and car loans. Mortgages and some student loans often have lower rates and may carry tax advantages or special protections depending on your country. You can include them later once higher-cost debts are cleared.

### Should I save or pay off debt first?

Many planners suggest building a small emergency fund first so you do not rely on credit during a setback. After that, high-interest debt typically deserves priority, since few savings accounts earn what a credit card charges. An employer retirement match is often worth capturing alongside debt payoff.

### Does paying off debt improve my credit score?

Paying down credit cards usually helps because it lowers utilization. Paying off an instalment loan may have a smaller or mixed effect, since the account closes. Consistent on-time payments matter most over time.
