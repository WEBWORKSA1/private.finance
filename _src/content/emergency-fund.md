An emergency fund is money set aside for one job: covering essential costs when something goes wrong, such as a job loss, a medical bill or an urgent car repair. It is not an investment and it is not a holiday fund. Its value comes from being there, in cash, at the moment you need it, so that a bad week does not turn into high-interest debt.

## Key takeaways

- A common guideline is three to six months of essential expenses, not three to six months of total income.
- People with variable income, single-income households and those with dependants often aim for six to twelve months.
- Keep the money safe, liquid and separate from your everyday account, ideally earning a competitive rate of interest.
- The best account type depends on where you live: high-yield savings in the US, a TFSA or high-interest savings account in Canada, easy-access savings or a cash ISA in the UK, sweep-in deposits or liquid funds in India, and high-interest savings or a mortgage offset account in Australia.
- Build it in stages, starting with a small first target, and automate the contributions.

## Why an emergency fund matters

Most setbacks are ordinary expenses arriving at the wrong time. Without a cash buffer, the default options are a credit card, an overdraft or a personal loan, all of which typically charge far more interest than savings pay.

It also protects your long-term plans: a cash reserve means you are not forced to sell investments during a downturn just to pay rent, and a few months of runway lets you look for the right job rather than the first one available.

## How much should you save?

### Start with essential expenses

The figure that matters is what you must spend each month to keep your life running, not your salary. Essential expenses usually include:

- Rent or mortgage payments
- Utilities, phone and internet
- Groceries and basic household supplies
- Transport needed for work and daily life
- Insurance premiums
- Minimum payments on any debts
- Childcare, prescriptions and other non-negotiable costs

Leave out discretionary spending such as dining out and travel, which most people cut first in a genuine emergency.

### Pick a number of months

Three to six months of essential expenses is a widely used guideline, but your situation should move you up or down that range.

| Situation | Typical target |
|---|---|
| Stable salaried job, dual income, no dependants | 3 months |
| Stable job, single income or with dependants | 4–6 months |
| Self-employed, freelance, commission or seasonal income | 6–12 months |
| Nearing retirement or in a specialised field where job searches take longer | 6–12 months |
| Homeowner with an older property or vehicle | Toward the higher end of your range |

Strong unemployment insurance or generous sick pay may justify the lower end; limited or slow benefits argue for more.

## Worked example: sizing and building the fund

Consider a household with the following essential monthly costs:

| Expense | Monthly cost |
|---|---|
| Rent | 1,600 |
| Utilities | 200 |
| Groceries | 500 |
| Transport | 300 |
| Insurance | 150 |
| Minimum debt payments | 250 |
| Phone and internet | 100 |
| Medication and other essentials | 100 |
| **Total** | **3,200** |

- A three-month fund would be 3 × 3,200 = 9,600.
- A six-month fund would be 6 × 3,200 = 19,200.

Suppose this household has one stable salary and one part-time income, so it settles on four months: 12,800.

If it can automatically transfer 500 a month, plus put a one-off 1,500 tax refund toward the goal, the timeline looks like this:

- Starter goal of one month (3,200): reached in roughly four months, including the refund.
- Full goal of 12,800: reached in roughly 23 months, before any interest earned.

Raising the monthly transfer to 700 would cut the full timeline to roughly 17 months.

## Where to keep your emergency fund

The ideal home for this money is safe, quick to access and earning some interest. That rules out shares, which can fall just when you need them, and your everyday account, where the money is easy to spend by accident.

Look for an account covered by your country's deposit protection scheme, such as the FDIC in the US, CDIC in Canada, the FSCS in the UK, DICGC in India and the Financial Claims Scheme in Australia. Each scheme covers deposits up to a set limit per person per institution, so check the current limit if your balance is large.

### United States

High-yield savings accounts, often offered by online banks, typically pay considerably more than traditional savings accounts. Money market deposit accounts and short-term Treasury bills are other options for part of a larger fund.

### Canada

High-interest savings accounts are the straightforward choice. A Tax-Free Savings Account (TFSA) can also hold cash or cash-like investments, and interest earned inside it is not taxed. Withdrawals are tax-free, but the amount withdrawn is only added back to your contribution room the following calendar year, so re-contributing too early can trigger a penalty.

### United Kingdom

Easy-access savings accounts let you withdraw without notice. A cash ISA shelters interest from tax, which matters most for higher-rate taxpayers or larger balances, since many people already earn some interest tax-free under the Personal Savings Allowance. Choose an easy-access or flexible ISA, and check current allowance rules, which have been changing.

### India

Many banks offer sweep-in or auto-sweep facilities that move surplus savings balances into fixed deposits earning a higher rate while keeping the money accessible. Liquid mutual funds are another common choice; redemptions are typically processed by the next business day. They are low-risk but not guaranteed, and are taxed differently from bank interest.

### Australia

High-interest savings accounts are common, though many require a minimum monthly deposit to earn the bonus rate. Homeowners can park the fund in a mortgage offset account, where every dollar reduces the interest charged on the loan.

## How to build it step by step

1. **Calculate your essential monthly expenses.** Use three months of bank statements to get a realistic figure.
2. **Set a starter goal.** One month of essentials, or a smaller fixed amount, gives you early momentum.
3. **Open a separate account.** Name it clearly so it feels off-limits for everyday spending.
4. **Automate a transfer on payday.** Treat it like a bill. Even a modest amount builds steadily.
5. **Direct windfalls to the fund.** Tax refunds, bonuses and gifts can shorten the timeline dramatically.
6. **Raise the target as life changes.** A new child, a mortgage or a move to self-employment usually means a bigger fund.

If you have high-interest debt, a common approach is to build a small starter fund first, then focus on paying down the debt, then return to completing the full fund.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Basing the target on total income instead of essential expenses, which can make the goal feel unreachable.
- Investing the fund in shares or other assets that can drop in value.
- Keeping it in your current account, where it gradually gets spent.
- Using it for predictable costs such as annual insurance premiums or holidays, which belong in separate sinking funds.
- Locking it in a fixed-term deposit with penalties for early access.

## FAQ

### Is three months of expenses enough?

For someone with a stable job, a second household income and few dependants, three months of essential expenses is often adequate. If your income is irregular or you support others, six months or more provides a more comfortable margin.

### Should I pay off debt or build an emergency fund first?

Many people do both in stages. A small starter fund prevents new debt, then extra cash goes toward high-interest balances before the full fund is completed.

### Can I keep my emergency fund in a TFSA or ISA?

Yes, in both cases, as long as the money is held in cash or a cash-like product rather than volatile investments. In Canada, remember that TFSA withdrawals only restore contribution room the following calendar year. In the UK, a flexible or easy-access cash ISA avoids withdrawal penalties.

### What counts as an emergency?

A genuine emergency is unexpected, necessary and urgent: losing your income, an essential car or home repair, or an unplanned medical cost. Sales, holidays and planned purchases do not qualify; predictable expenses are better saved for separately.
